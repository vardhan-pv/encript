Academic Documentation

About the Data Encryption Standard (DES)

Historical overview, cryptographic mechanics, Feistel network architecture, and comparative analysis with modern standards.

1. Historical Origin

The Data Encryption Standard (DES) was developed in the early 1970s by IBM, based on the Lucifer cipher, and submitted to the National Bureau of Standards (NBS, now NIST). In 1977, it was published as FIPS PUB 46 and became the worldwide standard for commercial encryption and financial transactions for over two decades.

DES is a symmetric-key block cipher, meaning the same secret key is used for both encryption and decryption. Decryption is performed using the exact same algorithm as encryption, simply running the 16 subkeys in reverse order ($K_{16}$ down to $K_1$).

2. Why 56 Effective Key Bits?

Although a DES key is represented as 64 bits (8 bytes), every 8th bit (bits 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64) is designated as an odd parity bit for error detection. The initial key permutation (PC-1) discards these 8 parity bits, leaving exactly 56 effective security bits.

With $2^{56} \approx 7.2 \times 10^{16}$ possible keys, DES was considered secure against 1970s hardware. However, in 1998, the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) built the custom "Deep Crack" hardware machine, which brute-forced a DES key in 56 hours. Modern clusters or GPUs can exhaust the 56-bit space in minutes, which led NIST to formally withdraw DES in 2005.

3. The 16-Round Feistel Network

DES processes data in 64-bit blocks. The block is initially permuted (IP) and split into two 32-bit halves: $L_0$ and $R_0$. Across 16 rounds, the right half $R_{i-1}$ is expanded to 48 bits, XORed with the 48-bit round subkey $K_i$, substituted through 8 non-linear S-boxes (each taking 6 bits and producing 4 bits), and permuted through the P-box. The result is XORed with $L_{i-1}$ to form the new right half $R_i$, while the old right half becomes the new left half $L_i$.
